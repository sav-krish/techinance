## NOTE ON ACCURACY

Three claims in the source are wrong and the episode teaches the corrected
version. Flagged here so the difference is deliberate and traceable:

1. **"If you make more than $12,200 you must file."** $12,200 was the 2019
   single-filer standard deduction. The document quotes 2024 brackets, so the
   matching 2024 figure is **$14,600**. The episode uses $14,600 and says the
   number changes yearly.

2. **"Common standard deductions: IRA, HSA, student loan interest, business use
   of car or home."** These are adjustments to income (above-the-line
   deductions), not the standard deduction. The standard deduction is a single
   fixed amount set by filing status; you take it *instead of* itemising, and
   these adjustments can be taken as well. The episode teaches that distinction.

3. **"Itemized deductions: ... Gains from the sale of your home."** A gain is
   income, not a deduction. There is an exclusion on gains from selling a main
   home, which is the opposite mechanism. The episode omits it.

## Document 1: Taxes and Deductions

Taxes are mandatory payments to governments from citizens or businesses to help
fund public services. Income from any job, scholarship or grant is taxable.

Tax Day is April 15: the deadline to file on the previous year's income.

Filing threshold: above it you are legally required to file. Below it, if income
tax was withheld from your pay during the year, you likely qualify for a refund,
so it is worth filing anyway. Check with your employer whether they have been
deducting for taxes.

### Tax brackets

A bracket is the percentage of tax paid on a range of income. Reaching a higher
bracket does NOT apply the new rate to your whole income. The new rate applies
only to the portion of income inside that bracket.

2024, single filer:

| Rate | Taxable income |
| --- | --- |
| 10% | $0 - $11,600 |
| 12% | $11,600 - $47,150 |
| 22% | $47,150 - $100,525 |
| 24% | $100,526 - $191,950 |
| 32% | $191,950 - $243,725 |
| 35% | $243,725 - $609,350 |
| 37% | $609,350 + |

### Deductions, credits, refunds

**Tax deduction** reduces the amount you owe by subtracting from taxable income.
The standard deduction is set by the IRS and depends on filing status (age;
single, married, or head of household). If your allowable deductions total more
than the standard deduction, or you are not entitled to the standard deduction,
consider itemising.

Adjustments to income (source calls these "common standard deductions"):
money into an IRA, money into a health savings account (HSA, MSA, FSA, HRA),
student loan interest, business use of your car or home.

Common itemized deductions: donations to charity, home mortgage interest, losses
from disasters and theft, medical expenses over 7.5% of income.

**Tax credits** reduce the tax itself. Total tax is calculated first, then
credits are applied. Refundable credits pay out even when you owe nothing.
Nonrefundable credits cannot: owing $100 with a $200 credit claims only $100,
though some nonrefundable credits carry over to the next year.

**Tax refund**: money withheld from your paycheck for federal tax across the
year. Withholding is an estimate, not a calculation from your filing status,
deductions and credits, so overpaying produces a refund.

### Forms

- **W-2**: standard form from an employer, at the end of the calendar year in
  January or February, or when you begin the job.
- **1099**: used when you earn through app-based work such as Uber or Lyft.

### Filing

Third-party services (Credit Karma, H&R Block, TurboTax) offer simple, secure
filing. Filing directly with the IRS is possible but known to be complicated.

### The overall goal

Plan ahead. Aiming for a big refund is not a good strategy unless you have
"hidden" credits or are overpaying: the IRS can take up to a year or more to
process a refund, you earn no interest while waiting, and the money would
otherwise sit in a compounding high-yield savings account. After filing, start
planning the next year so you do not overpay.

## Document 2: Interest and Insurance

Interest rates determine the cost of borrowing and the growth of savings. A loan
charges a percentage fee set by the rate. A savings account or bond returns based
on its rate. A higher rate means paying more on a loan, or earning more over time
in savings.

**Simple interest** is calculated on the principal only, so you pay or earn the
same amount each period. **Compound interest** adds previously earned interest to
the principal before calculating the next period's return.

**Creditworthiness**, based on FICO score and bank statements, determines how
likely you are to repay. A good score and history can qualify you for a lower
rate.

**Variable** rates change over time with economic conditions. **Fixed** rates do
not.

- Student loans are usually fixed; some private student loans are variable.
- Credit cards often carry variable rates, which makes them harder to budget for.
- Mortgages and auto loans are generally fixed, though adjustable-rate mortgages
  (ARMs) move after an initial fixed period.

### Where rates come from

The Federal Reserve sets the target range of the federal funds rate (FFR), the
rate banks charge each other for short-term loans, and the discount rate, the
rate banks pay to borrow directly from the Fed. These are monetary policy tools
for balancing the economy and money supply. Neither is the rate you pay your
bank, but both influence it.

When prices rise faster than the Fed's 2% inflation target, spending power falls
and the Fed raises the FFR. Higher rates make borrowing and investing costlier,
lowering demand and, in theory, bringing inflation down. When unemployment is
high and spending is low, lowering rates makes borrowing easier and stimulates
growth.

### Insurance

Insurance protects against accidents, losses, injuries or property damage.
Common policies: health, auto, home, life, business.

Three components of any policy:

- **Premium**: typically the monthly cost. Priced on the insurer's perception of
  your risk of a claim. Auto considers age, location, driving history,
  creditworthiness and state. Home considers property value, belongings,
  location, coverage and claim history. Health considers age, sex, health status,
  coverage level and location. Life considers age, sex, health, tobacco use and
  coverage amount. Insurers price the same policy differently, so compare.
- **Policy limit**: the maximum the insurer will pay, set annually, per term, per
  loss or injury, or over the policy's lifetime. Higher limits usually mean
  higher premiums. For life insurance the maximum is the face value, paid to your
  beneficiary.
- **Deductible**: what you pay before the insurer pays a claim. It exists to stop
  large volumes of small claims. On $4,000 of damage with a $2,000 deductible you
  pay $2,000 and the insurer pays $2,000. Higher deductibles usually mean cheaper
  premiums.

Types:

- **Health**: routine and emergency care. Usually an annual deductible plus
  copays and coinsurance. Many preventive services are covered before the
  deductible is met. Available from an insurer, an employer, the federal
  marketplace, or Medicare and Medicaid.
- **Home**: protects home, property and belongings against natural disasters,
  theft, damage and vandalism. Earthquakes and floods need separate coverage.
  **Renters insurance** is a form of home insurance, generally required by a
  landlord; without it a landlord or mortgage lender can buy it for you and bill
  you.
- **Auto**: covers claims from an accident or damage to another vehicle or
  property, repairs, and replacement after theft, disaster or vandalism. Required
  by a lender on a leased or financed car.
- **Life**: pays beneficiaries on your death. Premiums run for life (permanent)
  or for a 10 to 20 year term.
